With growing awareness and concerns about AI, companies are scrambling to address a problem: how do they protect their content and intellectual property without hiding themselves from potential users and customers? The first tool most companies reach for is
robots.txt, a plain-text file at the root of a site that says who is allowed in.

This got me thinking. If millions of sites publish one, what do those files
actually reveal? Do they show how industries differ in how they are handling AI?
Do they accidentally expose AI partnerships or deals?

I pulled the robots.txt files from the top million websites to see if I could glean any interesting patterns from all these policies. Have companies gotten more clever at handling bots?

The answer is mostly no. In a sample of 356 testable sites that declared a GPTBot block, 279 did not block a request with the GPTBot User-Agent at the exact path they blocked. Some of the most repeated AI policies were boilerplate files copied from third parties or CDNs, and a few had contradictory policies.

I'm not the first person to look at these files. [FT Strategies](https://www.ftstrategies.com/en-gb/insights/robots.txt-as-strategic-intent-analysis-of-large-publishers-practices-and-policies) analysed 70 major
publishers and found a clear pattern: 60 of them restrict at least one AI
crawler, and 23 block the training crawler while leaving the search crawler some
access. However, FT Strategies stopped at just reading the robots.txt files. I wanted to actually test them and expand analysis across 1 million sites and many industries.

## How I did this

I started with [Tranco's list of the top million domains](https://tranco-list.eu/) and fetched the
robots.txt from 990,825 of them. For each file I recorded which crawlers it
excludes, at which paths, and whether it was copied from somewhere else.

Then I took 1,000 sites, looked at what they intended to restrict and crawled as a browser, as a generic crawler, and as GPTBot and comparing
what the server did with what the file said. Where a site blocked a specific
path rather than the whole site, I tested that exact path. If the page didn't
exist for a normal browser, I threw the case out rather than score it.

## The web says less than you would think

<Chart
  name="01_block_rate_by_industry"
  alt="Share of sites declaring AI restrictions, by industry, with 95% confidence intervals"
  caption="Only 14.6% of policy-observable domains declare a root-level AI restriction. News media is the outlier at 25.7%; most other industries sit between 5% and 13%."
  source="Tranco top 1M, Oct 2026 · policy-observable domains · n = 741,498; industry labels from PDL"
/>

Start with the most basic fact: most sites have nothing to say. Of the 589,347
sites that returned a valid robots.txt file, 108,421, about 18%, block at least
one AI crawler at the root. Across all 741,498 policy-observable domains, that
is 14.6%. Another 46,379 block only below the root, so 154,800 block AI
somewhere. The remaining 586,698 show no AI blocking: 434,547 served a valid file that allows AI, 106,871 had no robots.txt, and 45,280 served a malformed file. The other 249,327 domains are outside the observable set.

The spread between industries is the first real signal. News media declares
restrictions at 25.7%, roughly three times the rate of software, e-commerce,
education, or government. I was expecting a wave of companies protecting
themselves from AI, and there is, but it seems concentrated in one industry.

<Chart
  name="04_rank_curve"
  alt="Declared AI restriction rate by traffic rank, roughly similar across bands"
  caption="Popularity does not predict AI policy: the root-level restriction rate ranges from 12.3% to 18.9% across bands, with a bump at 400k–600k but no consistent gradient from most to least visited."
  source="Tranco top 1M, Oct 2026 · policy-observable domains · n = 741,498 across ten traffic-rank bands"
/>

The obvious objection is that media sites are simply the popular sites, and
popular sites have lawyers. But the rate does not rise steadily with
popularity. It ranges from 12.3% to 18.9% across the ten bands, with a bump in
the 400,000–600,000 range and no consistent gradient from the top hundred
thousand down to the millionth. Being popular does not make a site more likely
to write rules.

<Chart
  name="01c_industry_by_rank"
  alt="AI restriction rates by industry within three traffic-rank bands"
  caption="Robustness check. News media sits about 3x above every other industry in all three traffic bands, so the media effect is not a popularity artifact."
  source="Tranco top 1M, Oct 2026 · PDL-labeled domains · n = 274,314 policy-observable"
/>

The media effect is not a size artifact either. Within every size band, media stays far
above everything else. But size has its own independent effect. Smaller
companies block more: 11.3% for companies with 1–10 employees, falling to about
6.0% for companies with 10,001 or more, and that downward trend holds within
each traffic band. Both what kind of company it is and how large it is matter.
Popularity does not.

## When they do restrict, they restrict a lot

<Chart
  name="03_granularity"
  alt="Split bar showing whether sites that block AI also block citation crawlers"
  caption="Of 108,421 sites that block at least one AI crawler at the root: 62% block citation too, 38% block training only, and 329 (0.3%) block citation while allowing training."
  source="Tranco top 1M, Oct 2026 · root-level AI blockers · n = 108,421"
/>

AI companies split their crawlers in two. There are training crawlers, which
collect material to build models (ex. GPTBot, CCBot, ClaudeBot). And there are
retrieval crawlers, which fetch a page because a person just asked a question
(ex. OAI-SearchBot, ChatGPT-User, PerplexityBot). Restricting the first can help protect your
IP. Restricting the second can cost you page views and revenue.

I expected a clean pattern: block the training bots and allow the retrieval bots. What I found was mostly all or nothing. Of the 108,421 sites that block at least one AI crawler at the root, 66,740 (62%) block citation as well, 41,352 (38%) block training only, and 329 (0.3%) do the reverse: they block citation while allowing training. The reversal is rare, but not zero.

The real split is between sites that draw a line and sites that block the whole category. Most blockers choose the whole category.

<Chart
  name="02_bot_ranking"
  alt="Share of sites blocking each AI crawler, grouped by training versus retrieval"
  caption="Training crawlers are blocked at 6.9–11.9%; retrieval crawlers at 6.8–8.4%. GPTBot leads at 11.9%; Applebot-Extended is the lowest training crawler at 6.9%."
  source="Tranco top 1M, Oct 2026 · policy-observable domains · n = 741,498"
/>

The ranking tells a consistent story at the margin. The top of the list is
training crawlers: GPTBot at 11.9%, ClaudeBot at 11.1%, Amazonbot at 10.9%,
Google-Extended at 10.2%. Retrieval crawlers sit lower on average, but the
ranges overlap: ChatGPT-User is the highest retrieval crawler at 8.4%, above
Bytespider, ImagesiftBot, and Timpibot, while Applebot-Extended is the lowest
training crawler at 6.9%. Sites are more willing to block training than
retrieval, but the preference is not absolute.

But that preference is not the same as a two-tier policy. The per-site numbers are blunt. 62% of blockers also block citation. The "protect IP, stay discoverable" strategy is real, but it is a minority position, not the industry standard.

## The most common repeated AI policy on the web is a Cloudflare template

<Chart
  name="07_templates"
  alt="The most repeated robots.txt files, grouped by what they say"
  caption="Cloudflare's content-signal boilerplate appears on 29,041 domains, from two templates. Meanwhile 63% of policy-observable files are unique to a single site."
  source="Tranco top 1M, Oct 2026 · policy-observable domains · n = 741,498"
/>

This is a number I find really interesting (maybe biasedly because I work at Cloudflare lol). The most repeated robots.txt among the
top million sites is a block of [Cloudflare boilerplate](https://ap.org/robots.txt) which includes policies like search allowed and AI training
denied. It appears on 29,041 domains, in two near-identical versions, and it
represents about 3.9% of the 741,498 policy-observable domains.

That is one company's default, copied onto tens of thousands of sites. The next most common files
are unremarkable: ["allow everything"](https://ipify.org/robots.txt), ["block everything"](https://registrar-servers.com/robots.txt), a [WordPress default](https://suramericana.com/robots.txt), an
[old template from a decade ago](https://www.cam.ac.uk/robots.txt). How many of these were intentionally set up and how many are boilerplates the company doesn't even know is there?

Meanwhile, 63% of policy-observable files are unique to a single site. That is not a contradiction.
Cloudflare's file is the most repeated single file at 29,041 copies, but that is
still only 3.9% of the 741,498 policy-observable domains. 63% of those files appear exactly once.

All of this matters for what comes next. You cannot enforce a policy you didn't know
you had but with 63% of these files being unique, can it be assumed these are intentional?

This is the part I did not expect.

Robots.txt is voluntary. Nothing about the protocol requires a server to honor
it. It's a statement of intent and policy, not an actual security protection.

I ran two checks. The path-aware sample covered 1,000 domains. 525 declared an
AI block; 356 of those were testable at a loadable GPTBot-blocked path. A
separate root-test sample tested 697 sites that declared no AI restriction.
Sites with no file, a missing path, a blocked browser, or an unverifiable result
were excluded from the relevant check.

Of those 356 testable sites whose file disallowed GPTBot, tested at the exact path they
blocked, with a browser and a generic crawler as controls, 78% served the
content to a request identifying as GPTBot anyway. Only 12% blocked GPTBot
while allowing the browser and generic crawler. Another 10% blocked every bot
that wasn't a browser, so the GPTBot block was incidental.

It runs the other way too. Of the 697 sites in the root-test sample that
declared no AI restriction at all, 16% blocked an AI crawler anyway.

<Chart
  name="09_path_scope"
  alt="Stacked bar showing whether AI blocking happens at the root or below it"
  caption="30% of all blocking happens below the homepage. We tested the exact paths sites claim to block for GPTBot, and the GPTBot non-enforcement rate went up, not down."
  source="Tranco top 1M, Oct 2026 · policy-observable domains · n = 741,498"
/>

There is a detail that makes this worse and better at the same time. 30% of the
154,800 sites that block AI somewhere (46,379) do it only below the homepage. A
site leaves its front page open and fences off their archive, or images or
whatever they consider important.

I tested the actual paths. The GPTBot non-enforcement rate got worse, not better:
when I requested a path a site wanted locked, it still opened.

## Collection disclaimers

<Chart
  name="06_invisible_web"
  alt="Two bars: what sites declare, and what could actually be measured"
  caption="63,147 sites refuse to serve robots.txt at all; 173,539 were unreachable. 8.5% of the path-aware enforcement sample could not be verified because the browser itself was blocked."
  source="Tranco top 1M, Oct 2026 · n = 990,825 crawled; 741,498 policy-observable"
/>

None of this is a complete picture, and I want to be specific about where it
breaks.

63,147 sites of the 990,825 fetched, or 6.4%, refuse to serve robots.txt at all,
returning a block instead of a file. Another 173,539 were unreachable. In the
path-aware enforcement sample, 85 of 1,000 domains (8.5%) could not be verified
because the browser itself was blocked; the separate root-test sample had 220 of
1,000 (22%). I threw those out rather than count them as enforcement.

And the enforcement test measures User-Agent behaviour, not real crawler
identity. GPTBot was the test crawler because it is the most-blocked AI crawler
and gives the largest sample. The tradeoff is IP verification: OpenAI publishes
its crawler IPs, so a request with the GPTBot User-Agent from a random IP is not
the real GPTBot. A cleaner follow-up would test the exact
bot each site blocks, including a crawler that does not verify by IP.

## Hypotheses I couldn't prove

A few things looked promising and didn't survive contact with the data.

**Licensing deals.** I had hoped robots.txt files would reveal contractual
industry patterns. A deal would show up as a single-vendor exception: block
everyone except the company you signed with. The full sample has 26,422 files
that explicitly allow exactly one AI vendor while blocking at least four
training crawlers. But the pattern is not a clean deal signal. 23,684 of them
exempt ByteDance's crawler while blocking the other training bots, spread across
23,672 distinct files. That looks more like a mass-produced SEO pattern than a
set of negotiated contracts. The signal is intriguing, but it is swamped by template
spam, so I can't use it to identify deals.

**Jurisdiction.** I expected a European split, given the TDM opt-out in EU law.
Among countries with at least 2,000 policy-observable domains, the rate ranged
from 5.7% in Turkey to 13.1% in Poland. The US was 10.6%, Germany 9.8%, France
10.4%, the Netherlands 11.1%, and Spain 12.4%. No EU effect, no US effect.

**Enforcement by industry.** News media contradicts its own robots.txt most
often (35.5%), and software sits near the least (10.6%, roughly tied with
professional services at 10.0%), but the intervals overlap too much to claim
it. It's a suggestion, not a finding. A deeper follow-up analysis is needed.

## What this means

Robots.txt is a strange kind of document. It isn't a true security protection. It puts a company's position on the record, where partners,
lawyers, regulators, and AI agents can read it.

With the rise in awareness about AI, I expected clear and intentional restriction patterns, but this turned out not to be the case.
I also expected to see a majority of sites with strict enforcement controls, but this was also disproven. 

30% of the blocking I found was invisible to a homepage check. In the path-aware
sample, roughly three in four declared GPTBot blocks weren't enforced at the
User-Agent level. And the most common robots.txt file on the web belonged to
Cloudflare.

If anything, this shows that while some industries and AI power users like me may be hyper-focused on AI, most companies are still playing catch up.
